## Overview

The jackalope is a mythical creature of American folklore, depicted as a jackrabbit with the antlers of a pronghorn antelope or deer. The modern phenomenon traces to Douglas, Wyoming in the early 1930s, where brothers Douglas Herrick (1920–2003) and Ralph Herrick are credited with mounting a jackrabbit head to a set of deer antlers and selling the first specimen to Roy Ball of the La Bonte Hotel for ten dollars in 1932. The fabrication tapped a much older tradition: medieval and early-modern European naturalists illustrated the 'lepus cornutus' or horned hare in bestiaries and natural-history compendia, the Bavarian wolpertinger remains a related taxidermy tradition, and the Persian / Arabic al-mi'raj is a horned hare of medieval Islamic mythography. The Wyoming variant rapidly became roadside-attraction kitsch and a regional tall-tale staple, complete with claims of human-voice mimicry, lightning-fast running, attack behavior, and 'jackalope milk' with medicinal properties. A widely-cited biological cause for at least some original sightings is infection by the Shope papillomavirus (named for Richard E. Shope), which causes large keratinous, horn-shaped tumors to grow on the heads of cottontails and jackrabbits. Douglas, Wyoming has self-styled the 'Jackalope Capital of the World' since 1965, with a city statue, a 'jackalope hunting license' sold by the chamber of commerce, and repeated state-legislature attempts (2005, 2013, 2015) to designate the jackalope Wyoming's official mythological creature.

## Theories & Explanations

### Scientific

**Shope papillomavirus origin** _(Richard E. Shope)_

Pre-modern reports of horned hares are best explained by wild lagomorphs infected with cottontail rabbit papillomavirus, which produces large, horn-shaped keratinous tumors.

### Skeptical

**Acknowledged taxidermy hoax** _(Douglas Herrick (self-described), Wyoming state historians)_

The modern jackalope is a known craft fabrication originating with the Herrick brothers in Douglas, Wyoming in 1932, propagated by tourism, postcards, and tall-tale tradition.
